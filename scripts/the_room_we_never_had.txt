[cast: MUM=hero woman, ELLIE=daughter, SISTER=sister, LANDLORD=landlord, THE GIRL=villain ghost girl]

# Chapter One: The Room We Never Had

The first time my daughter mentioned the other bedroom, I thought she meant the cupboard.

We had moved into the house three days earlier. Two bedrooms upstairs, a bathroom, and a narrow landing where the wallpaper peeled in strips. I had counted every room before signing the lease. With what I could afford after the divorce, there wasn't much to count.

But on Thursday morning, Ellie came downstairs carrying a pillow.

ELLIE: The girl in the other room says this one's mine.

There was a yellow stain across the pillowcase. It smelled of rainwater and something sweet that had gone bad.

MUM: What girl?

ELLIE: She doesn't like her name anymore.

I checked the upstairs windows. Locked. I checked under Ellie's bed, inside her wardrobe, even behind the shower curtain. Then I took the pillow outside and put it in the wheelie bin.

By breakfast the next morning, it was on her chair.

Ellie was eating cereal beside it.

ELLIE: You shouldn't throw away presents.

I asked whether she'd gone outside during the night. She shook her head. The back door was still bolted at the top, well beyond her reach.

I told myself I must have brought it back in. Exhaustion could do strange things. For weeks I'd been putting milk in cupboards and forgetting why I'd walked into rooms.

That afternoon, I bought a new lock for the back door.

At bedtime, Ellie asked me to leave the landing light on.

MUM: Are you frightened of the dark?

ELLIE: No. She gets frightened when she can't find her door.

I sat beside her until she fell asleep. Then I examined the landing wall opposite her bedroom.

It was uninterrupted wallpaper from the bathroom to the stairs. Little blue flowers. Damp near the skirting board. No door, no handle, no gap.

I knocked on it.

Solid plaster.

I went downstairs, made tea, and called my sister. I was halfway through describing the pillow when three soft knocks came from above me.

Then three more.

They travelled across the ceiling, slowly, towards the stairs.

SISTER: Is that Ellie?

At the foot of the staircase, I could see my daughter's bare feet through the banisters. She stood facing the flowered wall.

MUM: Ellie. Come down here.

ELLIE: I can't. She's holding my sleeve.

Her right arm was stretched straight out. The cuff of her pyjamas pressed flat against the wallpaper, as if caught in a closed door.

I ran upstairs and pulled her towards me.

For one horrible second, the fabric held.

Then it tore.

We slept in my bed with the light on. Ellie lay facing me, her hands tucked beneath her chin. I watched the bedroom doorway until dawn.

Once, just before six, she spoke without opening her eyes.

ELLIE: She says you used to be nicer.

The next morning, I rang the landlord. He answered cheerfully until I asked about a third bedroom.

LANDLORD: There are two.

MUM: Was there ever another one? Before a renovation?

I heard him put something down.

LANDLORD: Has your daughter been scratching the wall?

I looked towards the stairs.

MUM: Why would you ask that?

LANDLORD: Children pick at loose paper. Keep her away from it. I'll come tomorrow.

He hung up before I could tell him we wouldn't be staying.

I packed two bags. While Ellie looked for her rabbit, I went upstairs to fetch her inhaler.

The flowers opposite her bedroom had changed.

Yesterday, they had formed neat little rows. Now a rectangle of flowers faced inwards. Inside it, the wallpaper bulged slightly, rising and falling.

I held my breath.

The wall kept breathing.

From downstairs, Ellie called my name.

ELLIE: Mum? She's got Rabbit.

A small voice spoke beside my ear.

THE GIRL: She can have him back when she comes to bed.

I left the inhaler and ran.

We drove to my sister's house forty minutes away. I told Ellie we were having a sleepover. I told my sister everything else in the kitchen while Ellie watched cartoons.

My sister listened without interrupting. Then she handed me her phone.

SISTER: Show me the house.

The rental listing was gone, but there was an old photograph on a property website. The front windows were painted green. A woman stood at the gate holding a little girl's hand.

The woman was my mother.

I enlarged the photograph until the girl's face became a blur of squares.

There was no mistaking the red coat. Mum had kept it in a box for years. She said I'd refused to wear anything else.

MUM: We lived there?

My sister sat down.

SISTER: You don't remember?

She was nine years older than me. When I was little, she had lived with our dad. There were whole years we had never talked about.

SISTER: You were five when Mum left that place. You had an imaginary friend. Afterward, you couldn't sleep in a room with the door shut.

From the living room came the soft click of a television switching off.

ELLIE: Mum, where did Auntie put the other bedroom?

We found her standing beside the kitchen door, looking at the wall behind us.

A little blue flower had appeared above the light switch.

My sister touched it. Blue colour came away on her finger.

Then something knocked from inside the wall.

That was when I remembered the game.

I remembered a girl sitting on a narrow bed, wearing a nightdress with sleeves too long for her hands. She had shown me how to fold paper birds. We had whispered after Mum went to sleep.

One night, she asked me to change places.

Only until morning.

I had agreed. Then I'd seen her smile, and I'd run out and pulled the door shut.

She had begged for hours.

Finally, she'd used my mother's voice.

My sister gripped my shoulders.

SISTER: What did you remember?

Behind her, the kitchen door opened onto a room I hadn't seen for thirty years.

There was the narrow bed. The damp pillow. Paper birds hanging from strings above the mattress.

Ellie's rabbit lay on the blanket.

And beside it sat a girl with my five-year-old face.

THE GIRL: You promised it was my turn.

My sister pulled Ellie back. I caught the door handle, but the girl had already stood up.

Her knees cracked as she straightened.

THE GIRL: I've kept your place warm.

The wallpaper spread across the kitchen tiles. Flowers appeared beneath our feet. Somewhere behind us, the kettle began to whistle, although my sister owned an electric one.

I remembered that sound, too. My mother's kettle. The house was building itself around us.

MUM: If I come in, you leave Ellie alone.

The girl looked at my daughter.

THE GIRL: She wasn't the one who promised.

My sister caught my wrist. I pressed my car keys into her hand.

MUM: Take her outside. Keep going until you reach people.

Ellie started crying when I stepped across the threshold. I wanted to turn around, but the girl was moving towards the door, watching my feet.

I sat on the bed.

For the first time, she looked happy.

She went out. The door closed. I heard my sister scream once, then the slam of the front door.

After that, silence.

I've tried the handle every morning since. I've counted the paper birds. I've learned where the plaster softens when it rains.

Sometimes I can hear Ellie through the wall. She's older now. She reads aloud when she does her homework.

Yesterday, she asked someone why there was a blue flower beside her wardrobe.

I put my mouth against the plaster.

MUM: Ellie. Get your auntie.

Footsteps approached on the other side.

Then my own voice answered, gently, from inside my daughter's room.

THE GIRL: Come away from there, sweetheart. That's just the pipes.
